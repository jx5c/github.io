Course Policies and Expectations

Course Project

The course project is a group project with up to three students per group (the same groups used for the paper discussions). Each project includes three components: a design (identify a security problem; outline the system, protocol, or approach), an implementation (a non-trivial technical artifact or prototype), and an evaluation (experiments, case studies, or adversarial scenarios). Deliverables: a one-page proposal (by mid-term), a five-page final report (by final), executable code on GitHub, and a 10-minute presentation (8 minutes + 2 minutes Q&A), which is a major component of the project grade. All members are expected to contribute meaningfully. Research-oriented projects are welcome — discuss with the instructor.

Weekly Paper Discussions

Each week everyone reads one required research paper (about 15 pages on average). Each group leads one discussion during the semester; slots were assigned from submitted group preferences by a seeded, publicly verifiable draw (see how the slot draws work). Most weeks have a single leading group (~25–30 minutes). Four weeks — 6, 8, 11, and 15, marked on the schedule — have two leading groups: Group A leads the required paper (~25 minutes) and Group B leads the companion paper (~15–20 minutes: present its key idea, then lead the contrast discussion). On those weeks, everyone else skims the companion's introduction and conclusion (~10 minutes) so the whole room can join both discussions. The reading list — every paper, a free PDF link, page count, required background, and discussion questions — is on the Paper Reading Schedule.

  • Everyone reads the Required paper before class; on two-group weeks, also skim the companion paper's introduction and conclusion.
  • Slot assignments for Fall 2026 are final and posted on the schedule. The Week 2 discussion was led by a volunteer group selected by seeded lottery; all other slots came from the preference draw.
  • Every non-leading group posts one discussion question (or observation) by 9:30 AM on the day of class.
  • Discussion leaders facilitate discussion rather than summarize; on two-group weeks, both leading groups read both papers and present the contrast between them.
  • Grading (10% of the final grade): Understanding of the paper (30%), Organization & clarity (20%), Critical analysis (25%), Discussion leadership (25%). Unless otherwise determined, all members of a group receive the same grade.

The purpose is to read technical security literature critically — not to reproduce the research. You are not expected to follow every proof; you are expected to say what problem a paper attacks, what its key idea is, and where it falls short.

Collaboration and Academic Integrity

Discussion and the exchange of ideas are essential to doing academic work. You are encouraged to consult with your classmates as you work on problem sets. However, after discussions with peers or staff, make sure you can work through the problem yourself, and ensure any answers you submit are the result of your own efforts. Cite any books, articles, websites, or lectures that helped you, and list the names of students you collaborated with.

Do not pass solutions to problem sets nor accept them from another student. Do not post course materials (problem sets, solutions, exams, etc.) to websites or course-content archives. It is never okay to look up solutions to homework problems on the web. No cheating or copying from previous semesters, other students, or the Internet will be tolerated. Any form of cheating will receive 0 points in that task and I will report the case to the University. If you are ever in doubt, ask the course staff to clarify. See the university's Code of Student Academic Integrity.

Generative AI Tools

You may use AI tools for learning purposes only — to generate ideas and insights, explore alternative perspectives, or help you understand a paper (explain a concept, unpack notation, check your reading). If you use AI in this way, you must:

  • Disclose how you used AI in your submissions.
  • Not copy/paste AI output as your answer; always verify results and understand their limitations.

You cannot use AI tools to get answers to assignments, projects, or quizzes, or to generate your critical analysis or discussion questions for paper discussions. (AI assistance in producing presentation slides is permitted with disclosure — but the analysis and the discussion leading must be your own.) AI tools, cellphones, and internet access are not permitted during exams.

Timely Feedback

Course staff will aim to return graded assignments, with solutions, to students within 10 days of when the assignment is turned in.

Ethics (From Berkeley CS161)

  • In this class, you will learn a lot about attacks. To defend against attacks, you must learn the techniques that attackers use.
  • It is usually okay to break into your own systems.
  • It is usually okay to break into someone else's systems with their explicit permission.
  • It is grossly unethical and exceedingly criminal to break into someone else's systems without their permission.
